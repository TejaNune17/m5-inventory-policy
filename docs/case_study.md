# Case study: reorder policy on Walmart M5 demand

Teja Nune. Every number below comes from `docs/results.md`, generated by `make report`.

## 1. Problem

A retailer must choose how much stock to hold for thousands of item-store combinations. Too little loses sales; too much ties up cash. The question: which reorder policy reaches a 95% service level at the lowest total inventory cost, and how much does a forecast-driven policy beat a naive fixed rule?

## 2. Data and limitation

Walmart M5 (Kaggle): 30,490 item-store series, daily units from 2011-01-29 to 2016-05-22, weekly prices, and a calendar. 59,181,090 daily rows, reconciled exactly to source. After launch, 59.53% of days have zero sales.

The data has sales, not stock on hand. Inventory is simulated against observed sales treated as demand. Where the real store was out of stock, observed sales understate true demand.

## 3. Method

1. **Segmentation.** ABC by revenue and XYZ by weekly demand variability, over the 52 weeks before simulation. A-class series are 11,025 of 30,490 and carry 80.0% of revenue. A stratified sample of 1,000 series is used from here on.
2. **Forecasting.** Naive, seasonal naive, 28-day moving average, ETS, and a global LightGBM model. Rolling origin, 4 folds of 28 days.
3. **Simulation.** Daily (s, Q) review with lost sales over 364 days, against actual demand. Three policies:
    - P0: reorder point and EOQ from the trailing 90 days.
    - P1: the same formulas from the full history.
    - P2: LightGBM forecast and its out-of-sample error, recomputed every 28 days.
4. **Costs.** All assumed: unit cost 70% of price, holding 25% per year, $20 per order, lost sales costed at lost margin. 17 scenarios cover service targets and lead times.

## 4. Results

**Forecasting.** LightGBM did not beat the simple models overall. WAPE was 0.759 against 0.749 for ETS and 0.751 for the 28-day moving average. It was marginally best only on A-class series (0.624 vs 0.627 for ETS). ETS fell back to the moving average on 2,720 of 4,000 series-folds because the series were too sparse. Both are far better than naive (0.993) and seasonal naive (0.882).

**Policy comparison** (base: cell service targets, 7-day lead time, sigma 1 day):

| Policy | Fill rate | Cycle service level | Avg inventory value | Total cost | vs P0 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| P0 naive | 97.06% | 63.5% | $183,368 | $125,117 | 0.0% |
| P1 static | 98.78% | 76.1% | $196,322 | $108,860 | -13.0% |
| P2 forecast | 99.29% | 83.5% | $206,646 | $106,184 | -15.1% |

P2 holds more stock than P0 but costs 15.1% less: ordering cost falls from $66,080 to $51,060, and lost margin from $13,321 to $3,604. Most of the gain comes from estimating on the full history (P0 to P1, -13.0%). The forecast adds a further 2.5% (P1 to P2).

**Lead time.** The forecast matters more as lead time grows. At a 3-day lead time (sigma 1), P2 is 18.6% cheaper than P0 and P1 is 17.1% cheaper. At 14 days, P2 is 13.0% cheaper and P1 only 8.2%.

**Service-level trade-off.** Total cost is nearly flat across targets. Order cost dominates, because EOQ produces few large orders for low-cost items. For P2, moving the target from 85% to 99.5% raises fill rate from 98.78% to 99.58%, while total cost moves between $106,160 and $107,279.

No policy reaches a 95% cycle service level. The best is P2 at a 99.5% target, achieving 91.7%. The normal-demand safety stock formula understates lead-time demand for intermittent series.

## 5. Recommendation

| Segment | Policy | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| All A cells, BX, BY, BZ | P2 forecast-driven | Lowest cost in AX, AY, AZ, BX, BZ. In BY it is $17 above P1, with fill rate 99.64% vs 99.21% |
| CX, CY, CZ | P1 static | Lowest or within $46 of lowest. The forecast adds cost without material service gain on low-revenue items |
| Any cell | Not P0 | On erratic series P0 fails. Fill rate is 86.60% in BZ and 87.44% in CZ, because a 90-day window with no sales sets order size to 1 |

To actually hit a 95% cycle service level, replace the normal-approximation safety stock with an empirical lead-time demand quantile. That would be the next experiment.

## 6. Limitations and next steps

- **Lost-sales assumption on censored data.** Simulated stockouts use observed sales, which are already reduced by real stockouts.
- **Single echelon.** No distribution center, no shared supplier capacity.
- **Assumed costs.** Unit cost, holding rate, and order cost are assumptions. The $20 order cost drives order sizes and makes the service-cost curve flat; a lower order cost would shift weight to safety stock.
- **Supplier variability** is modeled only as normal lead-time noise (sigma_L), with no disruptions.
- **Next steps:**
    - Empirical or bootstrapped safety stock for intermittent demand.
    - A P2 variant driven by ETS or the moving average, since LightGBM did not win on accuracy.
    - Croston-type models for Z-class series.
    - A sensitivity sweep on order cost.
